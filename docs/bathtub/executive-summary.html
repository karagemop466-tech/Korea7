Executive summary

The job, the numbers, and the one decision that matters

A trip lever is stuck inside the hidden waste-and-overflow plumbing behind the tub. Five or more licensed plumbers have already declined to force it from above. Every one of them recommended going in from below. Below the tub is a storage unit, so going in from below means opening a drywall ceiling — which is the point at which this stops being a repair and becomes a small construction project with a permit question attached.

1. What is actually wrong

The assembly behind the tub is a trip-lever waste and overflow. A trip lever on the overflow plate moves an adjustable linkage — two brass rods and a clevis — which raises or drops a hollow cylinder inside the tee that opens and closes the drain. The patent that describes the design states the failure mode in its own words: if the linkage is too short the cylinder will not seat, and if it is too long the cylinder cannot clear the port inside the tee. Either way, the part that has to come out is the part that is jammed. US 4,352,213 ↗

That is why “just pull it out from the overflow” has failed repeatedly. It also explains why the plumbers would not force it: the alternative to a clean withdrawal is a sheared linkage or a broken overflow fitting, both of which turn a service call into an open-wall job.

3. Who can legally do the whole job

Of 9 licence numbers checked against CSLB, 7 are active and 2 are suspended. The suspended two are worth knowing about: both present as normal contractors, and one of them markets a CSLB-plumbing-class licence number while the licence is suspended for lack of qualifier, lapsed workers' compensation and a cancelled bond.

On the “all in one service” requirement, CSLB's own guidance decides the answer: a B (General Building) licensee may contract for and oversee a project that needs more than two types of work, and may sub-contract parts of it. 4. The decision that matters

Settle the permit question before anyone opens the ceiling. San Francisco's plumbing code exemption list covers leak repair, unstopping, tap replacement and toilet replacement. It does not cover bathtub waste-and-overflow removal. The building code exempts wallboard repair from a building permit but explicitly re-imposes the plumbing permit. So the ceiling opening is not a loophole — it is the thing that turns a $135 adjustment into a permitted plumbing job with an inspection, and it is also the thing that doubles the price.

That gives three coherent strategies, and they should be priced separately rather than blended:
