Why it will not come out from above

Not opinion: the assembly is described in a granted US patent, in its own words, including the two adjustments that cause it to jam. This page exists so the owner can tell a credible approach from a hopeful one.

Primary source. US 4,352,213 A - Linkage adjustment arrangement for a bathtub drain assembly (Robert R. Watts; assigned to Woodford Manufacturing, later WCM Industries). Filed 1980-11-07, granted 1982-10-05, legal status: expired. US 4,352,213 ↗ Filed 1980, granted 1982, expired. Quoted below because it is the clearest public description of the geometry that is hidden behind the tub.

What is behind the tub wall

Overflow plate and trip lever

The visible chrome plate on the tub wall with the lever. Two screws hold it to the overflow fitting.

Why it matters here. This is the only part that is meant to come off from inside the tub. Everything downstream of it is behind the tub wall.

Adjustable linkage (two brass rods plus a clevis)

The patent describes 'an adjustable linkage, usually consisting of two connected brass rods and a brass clevis' connecting the drop cylinder to the trip lever.

Why it matters here. A long, thin, jointed metal assembly that has to slide out through the overflow opening. Brass and steel fasteners in a permanently wet drain are exactly where corrosion welds parts together.

Drop cylinder (plunger) inside the tee

'A hollow cylinder known as a drop cylinder serves as a valve ... located within the vertical portion of the tee.'

Why it matters here. The plunger sits down inside the tee, below the overflow. If it seizes in the bore, the linkage cannot be withdrawn because the plunger is the thing being pulled, and it is the thing that is stuck.

Tee, overflow tube and drain shoe

The drain tube from the tub drain connects to a tee; the vertical overflow tube connects the overflow port to the top of the tee.

Why it matters here. All of this is behind the tub apron or under the floor. This is the hidden plumbing the plumbers declined to force.

What that means for this job

  • The owner's description is technically coherent. If the plunger is seized inside the tee, there is no path that removes it from above without either destroying the linkage or breaking the overflow fitting.
  • The patent confirms the assembly is behind the tub wall by design, so an access panel or a ceiling opening is not a shortcut, it is the standard second route to a waste-and-overflow assembly that will not come out the front.
  • This is why the work splits into two very different prices: an adjust/repair attempt done entirely from the overflow opening, and a replacement that probably needs the assembly reached from behind or below.
  • It also means the owner should ask each contractor one specific question: 'if the linkage shears, what is your plan and who pays for the opening?' A firm that has not thought about that has not done this job often.

Eight questions that separate a good bid from a bad one

Ask every firm the same eight questions and compare the answers. A firm that has done this work will answer all eight without hesitation.

  1. Will you attempt the overflow-only route first, and what is the price if it works?
  2. What is the price if it fails and you must open the ceiling, and does that price include patching and finishing?
  3. If the linkage shears during removal, is that covered in your price or is it a change order?
  4. Do you hold C-36 and B on one licence, or will you sub-contract the drywall?
  5. If a plumbing permit turns out to be required for your scope, will you pull it, and what does it add?
  6. How will you contain dust in an occupied unit, and will you provide the EPA Renovate Right pamphlet to the tenant?
  7. Can you give me two references from occupied San Francisco units where you replaced a waste-and-overflow?
  8. Is your price a fixed bid or an hourly estimate, and what is the hourly rate and trip charge?
