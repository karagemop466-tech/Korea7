Method and evidence

The metric, verified against its published form

Distance-weighted Tversky, α = 0.2 (FP), β = 0.8 (FN), triangular kernel R = 300 m = 3 px at 100 m.

TP_w = Σ_g max_{x: d(x,g) ≤ R} p(x)·max(1 − d(x,g)/R, 0) FP_w = Σ_{x: p(x)>0} p(x)·(1 − max_g max(1 − d(x,g)/R, 0)) FN_w = |G| − TP_w DTI = TP_w / (TP_w + 0.2·FP_w + 0.8·FN_w + ε)

Two independent implementations are shipped. k7.metric.components and k7.metric.dti_bruteforce agree to 1e-9 on random inputs (5 trials, plus kernel-distance tests). An earlier version of the fast path scored soft predictions without weighting by their value — TP was wrong by up to 0.28 — and was caught by that brute-force test and fixed (IR-07).

No GitHub Release exists on any surviving account and no mirror is under the 100 MB limit, so none of it can be recovered from here.

Consequence: this submission uses no geophysics. It is a spatial prior, not a detection. That is stated in the run card, in the submission note, and here.

Experiments run, and one hypothesis that the data killed

Hypothesis 1 (rejected). That a catalogue-silent uniform lattice at 400 m clearance would beat the registry family. Under a dense extension model it scores 0.0004 against 0.525 for a dense halo. The margin is enormous and the hypothesis does not survive.

Hypothesis 2 (adopted). That the score is set by distance-from-catalogue and dot budget rather than by clearance. Supported by ρ = +0.503 (p = 0.001) on 38 scored rasters, and by the fact that the top nine cluster at 14–20 px median distance.

Hypothesis 3 (rejected). That the registry rasters are near-catalogue halos. They are not: 94 % of the best file's dots are beyond 3 px and its median is 19.65 px. The "halo" naming is misleading.
