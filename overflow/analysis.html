1. Technical analysis — what is stuck, and why

The assembly. A trip-lever bath waste is a two-opening system: the visible trip lever on the overflow plate is connected by a brass linkage to a hollow cylindrical plunger (drop cylinder) that slides inside the overflow pipe and seats/unseats the drain opening below. Official component names (Watco installation sheet): overflow plate, linkage, drop cylinder, overflow elbow + gasket, drain elbow, strainer body, tee (S1). Moen's guide confirms the pattern: "the lever on the overflow plate opens and closes the drain", with replacement units 90410/90480 and tubing not sold separately (S3).

Why it gets stuck. The linkage and plunger live in a wet, mineral-rich environment; deposits and corrosion freeze the plunger to the overflow tube wall and the plate screws to the elbow. Manufacturer-adjacent and licensed-plumber guidance (Lowery Plumbing S6, Mr. Rooter S7) describe the standard fix from above: remove the overflow plate screws, detach the clip, pull the assembly straight up with lubricant (not at an angle). Family Handyman calls the lever-style linkage "complicated" and documents the common professional conversion to a simpler lift-and-turn stopper with a blank overflow plate (S5).

Why force is excluded here. The same sources carry an explicit warning: if the assembly will not come free, pulling harder can snap the plunger rod off inside the piping, leaving the plunger unreachable and converting a repair into a full assembly replacement (S6, "significant risk that the plunger rod breaks off"; S7, "you could break the assembly, which may require professional help to fix"). This matches the field report on this unit: 5+ licensed plumbers have already assessed the assembly as too stuck to extract from above without risking the hidden plumbing (owner report, REQ-04).

Where the working joints actually are. The waste-and-overflow is fastened under and behind the tub: the drain shoe screws into the underside of the tub, the overflow elbow and gasket sit behind the tub wall, and a tee joins overflow pipe to drain pipe (S4, S1). Industry guides state plainly that replacing the full assembly "usually requires access from behind or below the tub" (S9) and that with clear access "via an access panel or from a ceiling below" the full replacement takes roughly 2–4 hours (S10). With a storage unit directly below and no access panel described, the practical below-access route is a drywall ceiling opening — exactly the fallback the owner's brief anticipates.

Technical conclusion. The situation is consistent with published practice: a frozen trip-lever assembly that cannot be withdrawn from above is resolved by replacing (or freeing) the waste-and-overflow from below/behind, which in this building means opening the ceiling below the tub. The "all-in-one plumbing + drywall" requirement is technically justified, not a preference: the ceiling repair is a documented separate cost line (S9) and plumbers commonly bring a separate contractor for access-related repairs (S8).

Findings

  • No price contradiction. All three sit inside or next to published bands for their own scope. The gap between $135 and $614 is scope, not disagreement.
  • Missing line item in all three: none of the quotes as provided states the drywall ceiling open/patch/texture/paint scope, even though the below-access plan makes it likely. Published guidance treats it as a separate cost that "can rival the plumbing bill" (S9). Action: request the drywall line in writing from every bidder before comparing totals.
  • Access premium is real: Angi's 2026 data explicitly notes fees rise when a plumber must cut through drywall to reach pipes (S8). A below-access quote at or slightly above EST-1's level would still be consistent with the market.
  • Repair-first sequencing is cheap to test: EST-3's adjust/repair scope ($135) is the only quote that attempts the lowest-impact fix. If the plunger can be freed from below without replacing the assembly, this is the least invasive path for an occupied unit.
