# Executive brief — bathtub wastewater overflow trip lever

## The situation in one paragraph

A trip lever is seized inside the hidden waste-and-overflow assembly behind a bathtub. Five or more
licensed plumbers have already tried to free it from above and declined to force it, because forcing it
risks shearing the linkage or breaking the overflow fitting — either of which turns a service call into an
open-wall repair. All of them recommended working from below. Below is a storage unit, so "from below"
means cutting a drywall ceiling. That is where the project stops being a repair and becomes a small
construction job with a permit question, a drywall trade, and an occupied tenant unit to work around.

## The headline finding

**The drywall is not the permit problem. The plumbing is.**

San Francisco's plumbing code lists what needs no permit: leak repair not requiring cutting into piping,
unstopping not requiring cutting into or removing piping, and replacement of taps, angle stops, hose bibbs,
roof drains and toilets. **Bathtub waste-and-overflow work is not on that list.** Separately, patching
wallboard is exempt from a *building* permit — but the same section re-imposes the separate plumbing permit.
DBI states the trigger plainly: a permit is needed before cutting into or replacing pipes, especially pipes
that will be covered by a wall.

So the sequence that feels cheapest — "just open the ceiling and fix it, no permit needed" — is the one that
carries the permit exposure. That is worth settling before anyone puts a saw to the ceiling, not after.

## The three strategies, priced separately

| Strategy | What happens | Permit position | Price known |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Adjust from above** | Free or re-set the linkage through the overflow opening. No cutting. | Closest to the code's "unstopping" language; still not a named exemption. | $135 quoted, unverified |
| **B. Replace from above** | Trim kit or full waste-and-overflow, through the overflow opening. | Appears to require a plumbing permit. | $550–$614 quoted, unverified |
| **C. Open the ceiling** | Same as B plus access opening, plumbing, patch and finish. | Plumbing permit appears required; wallboard patch itself exempt. | **No quote exists** |

Strategy C is almost certainly where this ends up, given five plumbers have already declined the from-above
route. **There is no price for it.** That is the largest open cost in the project.

## What to do next, in order

1. **Ask DBI the permit question in writing** before anything is opened. Permit Center help desk,
   628-652-4900, `permitsf@sfgov.org`. Keep the answer with the job file.
2. **Send one identical written scope** to the shortlist and re-quote. The three existing numbers are not
   comparable — two replacements and one adjustment — and none of them states the ceiling, the permit, the
   disposal or the tenant handling.
3. **Lead with Ace Plumbing & Rooter (#829071).** It is the only San Francisco firm verified this session
   holding C-36 plumbing and B general building on a single licence, which is what "all in one service"
   actually requires. Marin H2O (#839817) also holds both, but is based in San Rafael.
4. **Ask for the licence number on every bid.** It is required on a bid anyway, and it is the only thing that
   lets you run the check — which is how two of the nine firms here turned out to be suspended.
5. **Serve the tenant written notice** with date, approximate time and purpose, at least 24 hours ahead.
6. **Confirm the build year.** It is the one fact that decides whether the federal lead-safe rule applies to
   the ceiling opening, and it cannot be answered without it.

## On the confidential constraint

One internal scheduling reason was given for not pulling a permit this week, and it is **not recorded
anywhere** in this repository or on the site — not in the data files, not in the audit, not in the commit
history. Nothing was deleted from the analysis to protect it: the compliance pages state what the published
code says, forward-looking, and stop there.

That choice is not ceremonial. The analysis says a permit is probably required for the scope that involves
opening the ceiling. Publishing the reasoning honestly is the only way the site can still be trusted on
everything else. Working without a permit is a decision for the owner to make with that fact in front of
them, not a fact to be hidden in the research.
